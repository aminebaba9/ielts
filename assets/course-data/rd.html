    TRUE vs FALSE vs NOT GIVEN — the definitive guide

    AnswerWhat it meansKey test
    TRUEThe statement agrees with information in the passage.Can you find a sentence that says the same thing (possibly in different words)?
    FALSEThe statement contradicts information in the passage.Does the passage explicitly say the opposite of the statement?
    NOT GIVENThe information is not in the passage — not mentioned at all.Have you searched the entire passage and found no relevant information?
    ⚡ The Critical Distinction: NOT GIVEN does not mean "I can't find it." It means the passage is genuinely silent on the topic. If the passage says X and the statement says Y (the opposite), it's FALSE — not NG. Students who lose marks here are usually choosing NG when the correct answer is FALSE because they stop searching too early.

    📄 Academic Passage Extract⏱ ~240 words🎯 Topic: Environment / Urban Studies

    Urban areas across the globe are considerably warmer than their surrounding countryside — a phenomenon scientists refer to as the urban heat island (UHI) effect. This temperature differential, which can reach as high as 10°C in exceptional circumstances, arises principally from the widespread replacement of natural vegetation with heat-absorbing construction materials such as asphalt, concrete, and brick. These impermeable surfaces absorb solar radiation during daylight hours and release it as heat after dark, preventing the nocturnal cooling that characterises non-urban environments.

    The implications of the UHI effect extend considerably beyond physical discomfort. Higher ambient temperatures drive increased energy consumption, as households and businesses intensify their use of air conditioning, thereby contributing to elevated greenhouse gas emissions. Research conducted across multiple urban centres has established a positive correlation between UHI intensity and concentrations of ground-level ozone — a secondary pollutant associated with respiratory conditions including asthma. Epidemiological studies in several northern European cities have demonstrated that heat-related mortality rates in dense urban cores are measurably higher than those recorded in adjacent suburban zones.

    In response to these findings, urban planners have put forward a range of mitigation strategies. The most frequently proposed involves expanding the proportion of vegetated surfaces within cities — through green roofs, urban parks, and street-tree planting — on the basis that plant transpiration generates a measurable cooling effect. An alternative approach advocates replacing dark-surfaced road and roofing materials with lighter, more reflective equivalents, sometimes termed 'cool surface' intervention. Whilst both approaches have demonstrated effectiveness in localised pilot programmes, scaling either strategy to encompass an entire metropolitan area remains logistically complex and prohibitively expensive for many municipal authorities.

    ✗ FALSE

    The statement claims heat is released during daylight hours. The passage says surfaces "absorb solar radiation during daylight hours and release it as heat after dark." The passage says the opposite of the question — this is FALSE, not NG.
    Q says: release heat during daylight hours
    P says: release heat after dark

    ✦ FALSE
    ✗ FALSE

    The L-P-C-D Method

    L-P-C-D — 4 Steps for Every T/F/NG Question

    Module 1 Essential
    L
    Locate — Find the relevant part of the passage Underline the key noun or named concept in the question. Scan for it (or its synonym) in the passage. Don't read every word — scan for the concept first.
    Q: "heat-related death" → Scan for: mortality / deaths / fatal / temperature-related
    P
    Paraphrase — Match the question to the passage Identify how the question rephrases what the passage says. Questions always use different words. Map the synonyms before deciding the answer.
    "financially viable" ↔ NOT "prohibitively expensive" → this is a contradiction = FALSE
    C
    Compare — Does the statement match, contradict, or go beyond? Match = TRUE. Contradiction = FALSE. Passage says nothing about it = NOT GIVEN. The question is: can you find clear contradiction, or simply no information?
    If passage says "after dark" and question says "during daylight" → CONTRADICTION = FALSE
    D
    Decide — and resist your general knowledge Base your answer ONLY on the passage. Something you know to be true in the real world is NOT GIVEN if the passage doesn't say it. Do not add outside knowledge.
    Even if green roofs obviously predate UHI research — the passage doesn't say this. Answer: NOT GIVEN.

    The TRUE / FALSE / NOT GIVEN Decision Tree

    ✅ Choose TRUE when

    • You found a sentence that says the same thing
    • The difference is only synonyms / paraphrase
    • Numbers and figures match exactly
    • The passage confirms the statement's claim

    ✗ Choose FALSE when

    • The passage explicitly says the opposite
    • The passage gives a different number or fact
    • The passage restricts something the question expands (or vice versa)
    • Words like "only," "always," "never" are contradicted

    ? Choose NOT GIVEN when

    • → The topic in the question does not appear anywhere in the passage
    • → The passage mentions the topic but says nothing about the specific aspect asked
    • → You would need to make an inference or use outside knowledge to answer
    • → The question asks about a time, origin, or comparison the passage never discusses
    ⏱ Timing rule: Spend no more than 90 seconds per T/F/NG question. If you cannot locate evidence after a thorough scan, it is likely NOT GIVEN. Move on — do not dwell.

    Time Management Strategy — The 20-20-20 Rule & Skimming Mastery
    Most fail Reading not because of English, but because of time
    ⏱ Timing

    Why 70% Don't Finish Reading

    Reading is 60 minutes, 3 passages, 40 questions. Average: 1.5 min per question. If you spend 3 min on one hard question, you steal from easy ones.

    ⏱
    20-20-20 Rule for True / False / Not Given: Passage 1 (easiest): 20 min max (including transfer). Passage 2: 20 min. Passage 3 (hardest): 20 min. Set timer: at 20 min, MOVE ON even if 2 questions left. Those 2 hard questions cost you 4 easy ones in next passage. For question types: True / False / Not Given — allocate 1 min per question, if stuck >90 sec, mark, guess, move.

    Skimming (3 min per passage)

    • Read title, first sentence of each paragraph
    • Underline proper nouns, numbers, italic
    • Note paragraph topics in margin (1-2 words)
    • Don't read details yet — just map

    Scanning for True / False / Not Given

    • Extract keywords from question (not all words)
    • Predict synonyms: "increase" → rise, surge, grow
    • Scan for those synonyms, not exact words
    • Read 2 sentences before/after match for context
    ⚡ True / False / Not Given Time Hack: For True / False / Not Given, do questions in order of difficulty within passage: if True / False / Not Given is hard, do easier types first to build passage familiarity, then return. Never spend >2 min on one True / False / Not Given question on first pass.

    Trap Avoidance & Keyword Strategy for True / False / Not Given
    Cambridge designs 3 trap types — learn to spot them
    🚨 Traps

    The 3 Trap Types in True / False / Not Given

    1
    Word Matching Trap: Question uses same words as passage but different meaning. Example: Question says "The study proved that..." Passage says "The study suggested that..." Proved ≠ suggested. For True / False / Not Given, always check verbs, qualifiers (all, some, most, always).
    2
    Extreme Language Trap: Options with "always, never, all, only, completely" are often FALSE/NG because passage uses hedging: "may, might, sometimes, tends to". For True / False / Not Given, if question says "always" but passage says "often", answer is FALSE/NO.
    3
    Half-Truth Trap: Half of statement matches passage, half contradicts or not mentioned. For True / False / Not Given, every part must match. If one detail is opposite, whole answer is FALSE/NO. If one detail not mentioned, NOT GIVEN.
    ⚡ Keyword Strategy for True / False / Not Given: 1) Underline keywords in question. 2) Predict synonyms (e.g., "cause" → lead to, result in, trigger). 3) Scan passage for synonyms, not exact words. 4) When you find match, underline evidence and write question number next to it in passage. This creates answer key map for checking.

    ⚡ Speed

    The 4-Step Evidence Location for True / False / Not Given

    1
    Question Analysis (20 sec): Underline 2-3 keywords (nouns, verbs, numbers). Ignore articles, be, have. Predict what answer will look like: number? name? reason? For True / False / Not Given, note if question asks for reason, example, result.
    2
    Synonym Prediction (10 sec): Brainstorm 2 synonyms for each keyword. "Increase" → rise, grow, surge. "Important" → crucial, vital, significant. Cambridge always paraphrases — they never use same words.
    3
    Scanning (30 sec): Move eyes quickly, look for numbers, capital letters, synonyms. Don't read every word. When you spot potential match, stop, read that sentence + one before + one after.
    4
    Verification (20 sec): Does evidence 100% match question? Check every word. For True / False / Not Given, write question number next to evidence in passage. This creates map for final check and prevents same evidence used twice incorrectly.
    ⚡ Practice Drill for True / False / Not Given: Take a passage, set timer 1 min per question. Force yourself to move after 1 min even if unsure. After test, review: where did you waste time? Usually it's re-reading same paragraph 3 times. Train to locate once, decide, move. Speed comes from decision, not reading faster.

    📄 Practice Passage — Coral Reef Decline

    Ocean temperatures have risen by an average of 0.13°C per decade since 1901, a rate that scientists consider unprecedented in recorded history. This warming has had a disproportionate impact on coral reef ecosystems, which are highly sensitive to temperature fluctuations. When water temperatures remain elevated for extended periods, corals expel the symbiotic algae that live in their tissues — a process known as bleaching — which deprives them of their primary energy source and renders them vulnerable to disease and death. Scientists estimate that over 50% of the world's coral reefs have already been lost since the 1950s, primarily due to warming, pollution, and coastal development. Restoration projects, involving the transplantation of laboratory-grown coral fragments onto degraded reefs, have shown promise in some regions but are currently too small in scale to reverse large-scale reef decline.

    Questions: 1. Ocean temperatures have increased by more than 0.13°C per decade since 1901. · 2. Coral bleaching occurs when corals absorb too much sunlight. · 3. More than half of all coral reefs worldwide have been lost since the 1950s. · 4. Restoration projects have proven effective on a global scale. · 5. The rate of ocean warming since 1901 is described as having no historical precedent.

    Answers

    1. FALSE "risen by an average of 0.13°C" — the question says "more than 0.13°C" which contradicts the passage's exact figure.
    2. FALSE Bleaching is caused by elevated temperature, not sunlight absorption. The question contradicts the stated cause.
    3. TRUE "over 50%" = "more than half." Direct paraphrase confirmation.
    4. NOT GIVEN Projects have "shown promise in some regions" but "too small in scale" — global effectiveness is not confirmed or denied.
    5. TRUE "unprecedented in recorded history" = "no historical precedent." Direct paraphrase.

    • I located the relevant sentence before deciding my answer.
    • I identified the paraphrase relationship between the question and the passage.
    • I chose FALSE only when the passage explicitly contradicted the statement.
    • I only chose NOT GIVEN after genuinely searching the whole passage.
    • I did not use my general knowledge — only the passage.

    Why Matching Headings is hard

    ⚠️
    The trap: headings that mention something in the paragraph — but not the main idea — Distractors use words that appear in the paragraph but represent only a detail. The correct heading always captures the paragraph's overall purpose, not a single sentence.

    AScientific inquiry into sleep remained largely peripheral to mainstream medicine until the mid-twentieth century. The invention of the electroencephalograph (EEG) in the 1920s provided researchers with the first tool capable of recording the brain's electrical activity during rest, but it was not until 1953 that researchers at the University of Chicago identified rapid eye movement (REM) sleep as a distinct phase of the sleep cycle. This discovery transformed sleep from a passive biological state into an active neurological process worthy of dedicated scientific attention.

    BContemporary sleep science is largely founded on the two-process model, first proposed in 1982. This model holds that sleep timing and duration are regulated by the interaction of two independent mechanisms: the homeostatic process, which tracks accumulated wakefulness and progressively builds pressure to sleep; and the circadian process, which generates an approximately 24-hour biological rhythm governed by environmental light cues. It is the interplay between these two drives that determines when humans feel compelled to sleep and when they feel alert.

    CAmong the stages of the sleep cycle, REM sleep has attracted particular scientific attention due to its proposed role in memory consolidation and emotional regulation. During REM phases, the brain processes and integrates newly acquired information, transferring it from short-term to long-term memory storage. Beyond memory, REM sleep is believed to facilitate emotional recalibration: the brain revisits emotionally charged experiences in a neurochemical environment depleted of stress-related neurotransmitters, potentially reducing the psychological intensity of distressing memories.

    DThe cognitive consequences of sleep deprivation are both well-documented and consistently underestimated by those who experience them. Impairments in attention, working memory, and executive function become measurable after as little as 17 hours without sleep. Crucially, individuals who regularly obtain fewer than six hours per night consistently overestimate their own cognitive performance, suggesting that chronic fatigue diminishes not only mental capacity but also the awareness needed to recognise that impairment.

    EPerhaps the most practically significant development in recent sleep research is the growing recognition of substantial individual variation in sleep requirements. The commonly cited figure of eight hours per night conceals a distribution in which some adults function optimally on as few as six hours, whilst others require nine or more. Emerging evidence suggests that a portion of this variation is genetically determined, with specific mutations in circadian clock genes associated with consistently shorter or longer sleep needs.

    The T-S-E Method for Matching Headings

    T-S-E — Topic Sentence, Skim, Eliminate

    Module 2 Essential
    T
    Topic Sentence — Read paragraph's first AND last sentence onlyDo not read the whole paragraph first. The first sentence introduces the topic; the last confirms it. These two sentences reveal ~80% of what you need.
    Para D: First: "cognitive consequences... consistently underestimated" / Last: "diminishes not only mental capacity but also the awareness needed to recognise impairment" → main idea: unawareness of cognitive decline
    S
    Skim for the central theme of the paragraphAfter reading first/last, skim the whole paragraph for the one concept that appears most. That concept is the main idea. Supporting details ≠ main idea.
    Para C: memory + REM appears 3 times. Emotion appears twice. Combined = the main idea. Physical health = not mentioned.
    E
    Eliminate distractor headings confidentlyCross out headings that mention topics not in the paragraph, or that describe only one sentence (a detail). Eliminate before you confirm.
    Para A: "limitations of early instruments" — EEG is mentioned but no limitations described → eliminate ii before choosing iii.

    How IELTS Constructs Distractors

    ❌ Distractor Type 1: Detail headings

    • The heading mentions something real in the paragraph — but it's only ONE detail, not the main idea
    • Para A mentions the EEG → Heading ii references early instruments → DISTRACTOR
    • Rule: if the heading only matches one sentence, it's a distractor

    ❌ Distractor Type 2: Adjacent-topic headings

    • The heading sounds related but covers a topic that ISN'T in the passage at all
    • Para C (memory/emotion) → Heading vii (physical health risks) → adjacent topic = DISTRACTOR
    • Rule: if the heading introduces a concept absent from the paragraph, eliminate
    ⏱ Timing: Allow 12 minutes maximum for a 6–8 question Matching Headings section. That's roughly 1.5 minutes per paragraph. Don't get stuck — make your best decision and move on. Wrong answers don't cancel correct ones.

    The G-M Double-Check Method

    G-M — Grammar Check, then Meaning Check

    Module 7 Essential
    G
    Grammar Check — does the ending complete the sentence correctly?Read the beginning + ending together aloud (in your head). Check subject-verb agreement, tense consistency, and that clause connectors (because/although/which) make grammatical sense.

    Meaning Check — does the resulting sentence match the passage?Even if grammar is perfect, verify the CONTENT is accurate. This is where most distractors fail — they sound right but say something the passage doesn't support or actively contradicts.

    Type 1: Grammar OK, meaning wrong

    • The ending reads smoothly but contradicts or misrepresents the passage

    • Always cross-check against the specific passage sentence

    Type 2: Meaning plausible, grammar wrong

    • The content sounds reasonable but doesn't grammatically fit the sentence stem
    • Watch for singular/plural mismatches and verb tense clashes
    • Read the full combined sentence aloud to catch these

    A-S-T — 3 Steps for Diagram Labelling
