    The four essay types you'll meet at university

    TypeWhat it asks you to doTypical prompt verbs
    AnalyticalBreak a text, event, or idea into its component parts to examine how they function and relate.analyze, examine, explore, investigate
    ArgumentativeTake a clear, defensible position and support it with evidence and reasoning.argue, evaluate, assess, to what extent
    ExpositoryExplain a topic clearly and objectively, without necessarily arguing a position.explain, describe, define, discuss
    Compare-ContrastExamine the similarities and/or differences between two or more subjects.compare, contrast, distinguish
    ⚡ Why this matters first: A brilliantly argued essay written in response to a prompt that actually asked for objective analysis will lose marks — not for the writing itself, but for answering the wrong question. Identifying the type is a five-second check that protects everything you write afterward.

    Authentic Prompt & Type Analysis
    📖 Module 1
    📝 Sample University Assignment Prompt

    "Argue whether the invention of the printing press was the single most transformative technological development of the early modern period."

    Reading the prompt like a writer, not a reader

    🔍
    Step 1 — Find the verb. "Argue whether" is unambiguous: this is an Argumentative essay. You are being asked to take and defend a position, not simply describe the printing press's history.
    🎯
    Step 2 — Find the scope. "The single most transformative" is a superlative claim — you must not only argue the printing press mattered, but that it mattered more than any competing development. This raises the bar for your evidence.
    ⚖️
    Step 3 — Anticipate the counter-view. Because this is a superlative claim, you already know you'll need to acknowledge competing candidates (gunpowder, the compass, shipbuilding) before explaining why your choice still wins. Planning this in advance is far more efficient than discovering it mid-paragraph.
    Common misreading: A student who simply explains what the printing press did — without ever taking and defending a comparative position — has written a strong Expository essay in response to an Argumentative prompt. The writing may be excellent; the task has still been missed.

    ✒️ Model Opening
    📄 Response type: Argumentative🎯 Prompt: The printing press essay

    HOOK Few inventions in human history have reshaped the flow of information as decisively as the printing press. CONCESSION While other developments of the early modern period — the compass, gunpowder, advances in shipbuilding — certainly reshaped exploration and warfare, none matched the printing press's capacity to democratize knowledge itself. THESIS This essay argues that the printing press was indeed the single most transformative technology of the era, not because it invented new ideas, but because it fundamentally altered who could access and challenge existing ones.

    HOOK
    An engaging, broad opening claim
    This sentence earns the reader's attention with a confident, sweeping statement before narrowing to specifics. It doesn't yet reveal the argument — its job is purely to make the reader want to keep reading.
    CONCESSION
    Acknowledging the competition before dismissing it
    Because this prompt demands a superlative claim ("the single most"), the writer must show awareness of rival candidates. Naming them specifically (compass, gunpowder, shipbuilding) proves this isn't an oversight — it's a deliberate, informed comparison.
    THESIS
    Specific, arguable, and previews the reasoning
    Notice this thesis doesn't just state a position — "not because... but because..." previews the actual LOGIC the essay will use, giving the reader a roadmap for what's coming in the body paragraphs.

    The Planning Framework
    📖 Module 1

    The P-I-B-O Method — 4 Steps Before You Write

    P-I-B-O — Parse, Identify, Brainstorm, Outline

    Module 1 Essential
    P
    Parse the prompt word by wordUnderline the verb, the scope-limiting words ("single most," "to what extent"), and any specific texts, periods, or concepts named. Nothing in the prompt is decorative — every word constrains your answer.
    I
    Identify the essay typeMatch the verb against the four-type table. This determines whether you need a defensible position (Argumentative/Analytical) or a balanced, objective account (Expository/Compare-Contrast).
    B
    Brainstorm freely before judgingList every relevant idea, example, or piece of evidence you can think of — without yet worrying about which ones you'll use. Filtering too early kills good ideas before they're fully formed.
    O
    Outline before draftingTurn your best 3–4 brainstormed ideas into a one-line-per-paragraph skeleton. This is where you decide paragraph order — before you're emotionally invested in sentences you've already written.

    Why five minutes of planning saves twenty of rewriting

    ⏱
    The instinct to start writing immediately is strong — and usually costly. Essays without an outline tend to wander, repeat points, or realize halfway through that a stronger argument existed all along. A rough outline, even five bullet points, prevents almost all of this.
    Rule of thumb: If you cannot summarize your planned essay's argument in one sentence before you start writing, you are not ready to start writing.

    🎓 Thesis

    The 3 Thesis Types for Essay Types & Structure

    1
    Analytical Thesis (for analysis essays): "Although [opposing view], [your claim] because [reason 1] and [reason 2]." Example: "Although social media appears to connect people, it fundamentally erodes meaningful interaction because it replaces deep conversation with performative performance." Shows complexity, not simple agree/disagree.
    2
    Evaluative Thesis (for evaluation): "[Subject] is [judgment] to the extent that [criteria], as evidenced by [evidence]." Example: "Remote learning is pedagogically inferior to in-person instruction to the extent that it diminishes spontaneous dialogue, as evidenced by decreased student participation rates."
    3
    Causal Thesis (for cause/effect): "[Cause] leads to [effect] through [mechanism], resulting in [consequence]." Shows chain of reasoning, not just X causes Y.
    ⚡ Essay Types & Structure Thesis Test: Can your thesis be debated? If no one would disagree, it's fact, not thesis. "Climate change is happening" = fact. "Universities should prioritize climate literacy over traditional canons because..." = thesis. Add "because" clause to force argument.

    📚 Evidence

    The ICE Method for Essay Types & Structure

    Introduce, Cite, Explain — every piece of evidence needs all three.

    I
    Introduce: Don't start with quote. Set up: "As Smith argues in her study of digital communities..." or "The data on student engagement suggests..." Shows you control source, not source controls you.
    C
    Cite: For academic essay, use 1-2 short quotes max per paragraph, or paraphrase. Quote: "..." (Author, Year). Paraphrase: No quotes, but still cite. Always use signal phrase + citation. Avoid long block quotes — they suggest you can't paraphrase.
    E
    Explain: This is where 80% fail. After quote, explain HOW it supports your thesis. "This demonstrates that...", "The significance of this finding lies in...", "This challenges the assumption that..." Never end paragraph with quote — always end with your analysis.
    ⚡ Synthesis for Essay Types & Structure: Band A essays don't use one source per paragraph. They synthesize 2 sources: "While Smith emphasizes X, Jones highlights Y, suggesting that the reality is more nuanced: both X and Y contribute to..." Synthesis = higher grade.

    The 4-Pass Revision System for Essay Types & Structure

    PassFocusWhat to Check in Essay Types & Structure
    1. ArgumentLogicDoes every paragraph support thesis? Is there counterargument + rebuttal? Any logical fallacy (strawman, false dichotomy)?

    The three non-negotiable qualities of a strong thesis

    QualityWhat it meansTest it with
    SpecificNames a precise claim, not a general topic area.Could this sentence apply to almost any essay on this topic? If so, it's too vague.
    ArguableA reasonable person could disagree with it.Could someone credibly argue the opposite? If not, it's a fact, not a thesis.
    RoadmappedPreviews the reasoning or structure the essay will follow.Does it hint at HOW you'll prove this, not just WHAT you claim?
    ⚡ The single fastest fix for weak essays: If you're unsure why an essay feels unfocused, check the thesis first. A vague thesis almost always produces vague body paragraphs, because there's no precise claim for them to support.

    Weak vs Strong: A Direct Comparison
    🌱 Module 2
    📝 Sample Topic

    An essay evaluating whether city governments should prioritize funding for urban green spaces (parks, community gardens) as public health infrastructure.

    The same topic, two very different theses

    ❌
    Weak: "Urban green spaces are good for cities, and there are many benefits to having parks." This is not a thesis — it's a topic statement. No reasonable person disagrees that parks have "benefits." There is nothing here to argue, prove, or structure paragraphs around.
    ✅
    Strong: "Because access to urban green space directly reduces stress-related illness and encourages physical activity, city governments should treat parks not as optional amenities but as essential public health infrastructure, deserving of the same budgetary priority as hospitals and clinics." This makes a specific, arguable claim (equal budget priority to hospitals) and previews exactly how it will be defended (health outcomes).
    What actually changed: Notice the strong version didn't just add more words — it added a specific, contestable POSITION (equal to hospital funding) that a reasonable person could push back on. That contestability is what makes it a real thesis.

    ✒️ Model Thesis
    📄 Response type: Argumentative🎯 Topic: Urban green space policy

    REASONING Because access to urban green space directly reduces stress-related illness and encourages physical activity, CLAIM city governments should treat parks not as optional amenities but as essential public health infrastructure, SCOPE / STAKES deserving of the same budgetary priority as hospitals and clinics.

    REASONING
    The "Because" Clause Previews the Evidence Path
    Opening with "because" immediately signals to the reader HOW the claim will be supported — two specific mechanisms (stress reduction, physical activity) that will likely become the two body paragraphs.
    CLAIM
    The Actual Arguable Position
    "Not as optional amenities but as essential... infrastructure" is a genuine either/or claim — a reader could disagree, arguing parks ARE better understood as optional amenities. That disagreement is exactly what makes this a thesis rather than a fact.
    SCOPE / STAKES
    Making the Claim's Real-World Implication Concrete
    "The same budgetary priority as hospitals and clinics" translates an abstract claim into something specific and testable — a reader now knows exactly what policy the essay is arguing for, not just a vague sentiment.

    The Thesis Formula
    🌱 Module 2

    The B-C-S Formula — Because, Claim, Scope

    B-C-S — A Reliable Thesis Template

    Module 2 Essential
    B
    Because — state your central reasoningThis isn't required in every thesis, but including it forces you to know your OWN argument's logic before you write a single body paragraph.
    "Because remote work reduces commuting-related emissions and office energy consumption..."
    C
    Claim — state your specific, arguable positionThis is the sentence someone could push back on. If nobody could reasonably disagree, this isn't a claim yet.
    "...companies should be required, not merely encouraged, to offer remote work options..."
    S
    Scope — define exactly what the claim coversNarrow the claim to something provable within your essay's length — avoid claims so broad they'd need a book to defend.
    "...for all roles that do not require physical presence, within large corporations specifically."

    Placement: Where the Thesis Belongs

    📍
    Convention: last sentence of the introduction. This isn't arbitrary — it lets the introduction build UP to the claim through hook and context, so the thesis lands with maximum clarity after the reader already has the necessary background.
    Common error: Burying the thesis in the middle of a long introductory paragraph, or splitting it across two sentences separated by unrelated context. If a reader has to hunt for your claim, it isn't doing its job.

    Authentic Example & Funnel Structure
    🚪 Module 3
    📝 Sample Assignment Prompt

    "Evaluate the claim that standardized testing improves educational equity."

    The most common introduction failure: skipping the funnel

    ❌
    Too narrow, too fast: "Standardized testing has a Cronbach's alpha reliability coefficient that varies by subject area, which affects equity outcomes." A reader with no prior context is lost before sentence one ends — technical detail with zero on-ramp.
    ❌
    Too broad, too generic: "Education is one of the most important things in a person's life, and it has changed a lot throughout history." This could open literally any education essay ever written — it earns no specific attention and delays the real topic.
    ✅
    Correctly funneled: Opens with a specific, engaging claim about testing's promise (equalizing opportunity regardless of background), narrows to the actual debate (whether it delivers on that promise), then states a clear, arguable thesis. See the fully annotated version on the next slide.

    ✒️ Model Introduction
    📄 Response type: Argumentative🎯 Prompt: Standardized testing & equity

    HOOK Standardized testing was born from a genuinely equalizing promise: that a single, uniform exam could measure ability regardless of a student's wealth, connections, or the reputation of their school. CONTEXT In recent decades, however, mounting evidence has suggested that test performance correlates strongly with household income and access to test preparation resources, raising the question of whether standardized testing still serves its original equalizing function. THESIS This essay argues that, far from promoting educational equity, standardized testing in its current form largely reproduces existing socioeconomic advantages, and that genuine equity would require substantial reform to both test design and access to preparation resources.

    HOOK
    Specific Historical Framing, Not a Generic Opener
    Instead of a vague claim about education's importance, this hook names the SPECIFIC original purpose of standardized testing — giving the reader something concrete and slightly surprising (a "promise" that the essay will go on to question) rather than a platitude.
    CONTEXT
    Exactly the Background Needed — No More
    This sentence supplies the one fact the reader needs (the income correlation) to understand why the thesis is contestable, without wandering into unrelated history of testing or unrelated statistics that don't serve the argument.
    THESIS
    Answers the Prompt's Exact Question, With a Twist
    Notice the thesis doesn't just answer "does testing improve equity" — it adds a second, more ambitious claim (what genuine equity would require), giving the essay a natural second half to develop beyond simple critique.

    Hook Types Framework
    🚪 Module 3

    Five reliable hook types

    Choosing Your Opening Move

    Module 3 Essential
    1
    The Surprising Fact or StatisticA specific, verifiable number that reframes how the reader thinks about the topic.
    "Fewer than 3% of appeals against standardized test scoring errors are ever successful."
    2
    The Reframed AssumptionState a widely-held belief, positioning the essay to complicate or challenge it.
    "Standardized testing was born from a genuinely equalizing promise..."
    3
    The Illustrative ScenarioA brief, concrete hypothetical or real scenario that makes an abstract issue tangible.
    "Consider two students of identical ability, one with access to a $2,000 test-preparation course and one without."
    4
    The Direct QuestionUse sparingly — effective only when the question is genuinely open, not rhetorical filler.
    "Can a single exam, taken on a single day, fairly represent years of unequal educational opportunity?"
    5
    The Relevant QuotationA brief, directly relevant quotation from a credible, named source — never decorative or generic.
    A named education researcher's specific claim about testing's original design intent.
    ⚠️ Hooks to avoid entirely: Dictionary definitions ("Webster's defines equity as..."), universal statements ("Throughout history, humans have..."), and questions with an obvious answer ("Is education important?"). All three signal a writer stalling before saying anything specific.

    The Straw Man Trap
    ⚖️ Module 6

    Weak (straw man) vs strong (steel man) counterargument

    ❌
    Straw man — an easy target: "Some people think testing should just be abolished completely with no replacement, but that's clearly impractical." Almost nobody who supports reforming testing actually argues for zero assessment of any kind — this concedes a position no serious opponent holds, so defeating it proves nothing.
    ✅
    Steel man — the genuinely strong version: "Defenders of standardized testing point out that, whatever its flaws, it remains more objective than alternatives like teacher recommendations or extracurricular records, which are arguably even more vulnerable to socioeconomic bias." This is the actual best argument a thoughtful opponent would make — and it's genuinely harder to rebut, which is exactly why addressing it is more persuasive.
    Test yourself: Before writing a counterargument, ask: "Would a genuinely smart person who disagrees with me recognize this as a fair summary of their view?" If not, you've built a straw man.

    Informal vs Academic Register
    🧵 Module 7

    The same idea, two registers

    ❌
    Informal: "It's pretty obvious that testing isn't fair, and honestly, I think schools should just stop using it because it's really unfair to poorer kids." Contractions, vague intensifiers ("pretty obvious," "really unfair"), and an unhedged overclaim ("just stop using it") all undermine credibility here.
    ✅
    Academic: "The evidence strongly suggests that standardized testing, in its current form, disproportionately disadvantages students from lower-income households." No contractions, a hedged but confident claim ("strongly suggests," not "proves"), and precise rather than vague evaluative language ("disproportionately disadvantages" rather than "really unfair").
    Hedging is not weakness: "Strongly suggests" is often MORE credible than "proves," because it accurately reflects what evidence can actually establish. Overclaiming ("this proves") invites a skeptical reader to look for the one exception that breaks your claim.

    The Given-New Cohesion Pattern

    Module 7 Essential
    1
    Start with "Given" informationOpen a new paragraph by referencing something the reader already knows from the previous paragraph.
    2
    Move to "New" informationOnce the reader is anchored in familiar territory, introduce the paragraph's actual new content.
    3
    This "new" becomes the next paragraph's "given"Each paragraph's new information becomes the anchor point for the next — creating a genuine chain rather than a list.

    Four Transition Categories

    • Addition: furthermore, moreover, in addition
    • Contrast: however, conversely, on the other hand
    • Cause-effect: consequently, as a result, therefore
    • Sequence: subsequently, having established X...

    Register Red Flags to Edit Out

    • Contractions (don't, it's, can't)
    • Second person "you"
    • Vague intensifiers (really, very, a lot)
    • Casual connectors (so, plus, anyway)

    S-S-C — Synthesize, State Significance, Close

    Module 8 Essential
    S
    Synthesize — combine body points into a new insightAsk: what becomes visible only when these separate points are seen together?
    S
    State significance — answer "so what?"Connect the argument to a broader implication, without overreaching into an unrelated topic entirely.
    C
    Close — end on something memorableA crisp final image or idea, not a mechanical restatement of the thesis word-for-word.

    Two-pass revision: content first, polish second

    Pass 1 — Content & Structure

    • Does every paragraph serve the thesis?
    • Is evidence specific and properly explained?
    • Is the strongest counterargument addressed?
    • Does the conclusion synthesize, not summarize?

    Pass 2 — Sentence-Level Polish

    • Any contractions or casual language remaining?
    • Any overclaimed statements needing hedging?
    • Are transitions varied, not repetitive?
    • Read the essay aloud — any awkward sentences?
    Why two separate passes: Fixing commas while the argument's structure is still weak wastes effort — content problems often require deleting or reordering whole paragraphs, which would undo sentence-level polish anyway. Fix structure first.
