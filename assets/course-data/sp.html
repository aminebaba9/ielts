    Q: What do you usually do in your free time?
    Honestly, I'm quite a homebody at heart, so I tend to gravitate towards things like reading and cooking rather than going out. I've become a bit obsessed with cooking recently — I started experimenting with North African cuisine after watching a documentary, and it just sort of snowballed from there.     ✦ Band 9 · FC + LR
    "Homebody at heart" — Idiomatic Vocabulary
    Instead of saying "I like staying home," the speaker uses the collocation "homebody at heart." This kind of natural, idiomatic phrasing is exactly what earns top Lexical Resource marks. "Gravitate towards" is another idiomatic phrase used accurately in context.
    LR ↑FC ↑
    ✦ Band 9 · LR + GRA
    "It just sort of snowballed" — Natural Elaboration
    This phrase is authentic, informal, and completely natural — precisely what Part 1 demands. Notice the structure: the speaker gives a reason for the hobby ("after watching a documentary"), then extends with a consequence ("snowballed from there"). This is the ACEM technique in action.

    🎙
    Q: Has your hometown changed much since you were a child?
    Enormously, yes — it's almost unrecognisable in some ways. When I was growing up, it was quite a quiet, sleepy kind of place, but over the last decade or so it's been completely transformed — there's been a surge in construction, new commercial zones have sprung up everywhere, and the traffic has become absolutely relentless.     One-word affirmation + emphatic opener
    "Enormously, yes" is a natural spoken response that confirms the question and adds emphasis simultaneously. Starting with an adverb of degree ("enormously") before giving detail is a marker of genuine spoken fluency — it sounds real, not scripted.

    "A surge in construction / commercial zones have sprung up / traffic has become relentless" — three specific, parallel examples that show the change concretely.     The core techniques that move answers from Band 5–6 to Band 8–9
    🗣️ Part 1

    The ACEM Technique — Extending Every Answer

    Band 5–6 candidates answer the question. Band 8–9 candidates extend, explain, and exemplify. Use ACEM to build a full 4–5 sentence response from any simple question.

    ACEM — The 4-Step Answer Formula

    Part 1 Essential
    A
    Answer — Give your direct response Answer the question simply and directly. Don't overthink it.
    Q: Do you enjoy cooking? → A: "Yes, I really do actually — it's something I've gotten into quite recently."
    C
    Comment — Add a detail, reason, or feeling Why do you feel this way? Give the examiner something to react to.
    "I find it really therapeutic — there's something satisfying about making something from scratch."
    E
    Example — Give a specific, personal example Specific examples show Lexical Resource and make your answer memorable.
    "Just last weekend, I tried making a traditional tagine for the first time and it turned out surprisingly well."
    M
    More — Add one more dimension (contrast, comparison, future) Optional but powerful. Adds depth and shows spontaneous fluency.
    "Though I have to admit, I still struggle with getting the timing right — some things burn before others are even cooked!"

    5 Rules for Part 1 Success

    ✅ Do

    • Give 3–5 sentences per answer
    • Use personal, specific examples
    • Vary your openers — not always "Yes, I..."
    • Use natural hesitation fillers ("Well...", "I'd say...")
    • Show a range of tenses (past, present, conditional)

    ❌ Avoid

    • One-sentence answers ("Yes, I do.")
    • Memorised, scripted-sounding responses
    • Repeating the question back verbatim
    • Long pauses filled with "Ummm..." (10+ seconds)
    • Starting every answer with "Of course, ..."
    🎙 Pronunciation Tip: Examiners don't penalise accents — they penalise unclear speech. Focus on word stress, sentence rhythm, and not rushing. Slowing down by 10% often improves your score by a full band.

    Fluency & Coherence Mastery — Speak Without Hesitation
    Band 9 fluency is not speed — it's flow
    🗣 Fluency

    The ACEM / 1-Minute / PEEL Frameworks for Part 1 - Interview

    💬
    Part 1 — ACEM (Answer + Context + Example + Mini-conclusion): Target 20-30 sec, 3-4 sentences. Never 1 sentence. "Do you like reading?" → "I love historical fiction (Answer). I grew up in a house full of books (Context). Last month I finished Shogun, 800 pages in 3 days (Example). So yeah, big part of my routine (Mini)."
    🗒
    Part 2 — 1-Minute Mind Map: 4 quadrants: Who/What/When/Where + Feelings + Story + Vocab. No sentences, only keywords. Structure: Past → Description → Story → Feelings. Use "If I remember correctly..." to buy time. End with reflection: "Looking back, this taught me..."
    🧠
    Part 3 — PEEL + Critical: Point: "I think tech changed..." Explain: "Because..." Example: "For instance, in my country..." Link: "This suggests..." Add evaluation: "While some argue..., I contend..." Show ability to speculate, compare past/future.
    ⚡ Hesitation Fillers Band 9: Avoid "uh, um". Use: "That's an interesting question...", "Well, I suppose...", "Let me think...", "I've never thought about it, but..." Natural fillers give 2-3 sec without penalty.

    ⭐ Band 9 Model Answers — Authentic Questions real, recently-reported exam question

    🏫 Reported March 2026, Part 1 · Topic: Social Media

    🎙
    Q: When did you start using social media?
    Let me think... I'd say I first got into it around the age of fourteen or so, when everyone at school was joining one particular platform and I didn't want to be left out! To be honest, I was pretty late to some of the others — my parents were actually far more tech-savvy than me for a while, which is a bit embarrassing to admit. These days I mostly stick to just two apps; I've become a lot more selective about where I spend my time online.
    ✦ Band 9 · FC
    Natural Hesitation + Approximate Age
    "Let me think..." followed by "around the age of fourteen or so" sounds genuinely spontaneous — real people rarely recall exact ages instantly. This natural imprecision is more convincing than a suspiciously exact answer.
    FC ↑
    ✦ Band 9 · LR + FC
    "Tech-Savvy" + Self-Deprecating Aside
    "Tech-savvy" is a natural collocation used correctly in context. "Which is a bit embarrassing to admit" is a light, humorous aside — exactly the kind of spontaneous personality that examiners reward in Part 1.
    LR ↑FC ↑
    🎙
    Q: Do you think you spend too much time on social media?
    Honestly, yes, probably more than I'd like to admit! I think what happens is you open the app just to check one notification, and then twenty minutes disappear before you even realise it — it's designed to be addictive, if I'm being honest. That said, I have started setting screen-time limits on my phone recently, and it's actually made a noticeable difference. I still catch myself scrolling out of habit sometimes, though, especially when I'm bored.
    ✦ Band 9 · LR + GRA
    "Twenty Minutes Disappear" — Vivid Idiomatic Image
    This phrase paints a relatable, specific picture rather than a flat "I lose track of time." "It's designed to be addictive" also shows an opinion supported by a claim about the technology itself, not just personal habit.
    LR ↑GRA ↑
    ✦ Band 9 · FC
    "That Said" — the M in ACEM
    This contrast marker introduces the "More" step of ACEM — a nuance that complicates the simple "yes" answer, showing the speaker can extend an answer naturally rather than stopping after the first honest admission.
    FC ↑

    Why Part 2 is different

    🔑
    You control the content entirely — The examiner stays silent during your 2 minutes. There are no prompts, no help, no follow-up questions. This means your planning minute is critical. The POINT system on Slide 4 tells you exactly how to use it.
    ⏱
    2 minutes is longer than you think — Most students stop after 60–70 seconds, which signals they are running out of language. At Band 8–9, you should speak for the full 1:45–2:00 and the examiner will need to stop you. Practise with a timer.

    Authentic Cue Card
    A typical Part 2 cue card from a high-frequency topic area
    📋 Part 2
    Describe a person who has had a significant influence on your life.
    You should say:
    • Who this person is and how you know them
    • How long you have known this person
    • What qualities or actions made them influential
    • And explain how they have changed your way of thinking or behaving
    You will have 1 minute to prepare before speaking for up to 2 minutes.

    Using your 1-minute preparation wisely

    Do not try to write full sentences. Use single keywords or phrases only — one per bullet point. The goal is to trigger your memory, not to read from a script.

    📝
    Example prep notes for this card: (1) "secondary school teacher — Mrs Hassan" (2) "10 yrs" (3) "challenged me publicly / never let mediocrity slide / infectious curiosity" (4) "changed how I approach learning — effort > talent mindset"
    Tip: Choose the person you can talk about most fluently — not the most impressive one. The examiner is assessing your English, not your social network.

    ⭐ Band 9
    🎙
    Cue card: Describe a person who has had a significant influence on your life
    Introduction — who & how long The person I'd like to talk about is a secondary school teacher of mine — a woman called Mrs Bensalem, who taught me literature when I was about sixteen. I've known her for roughly a decade now, and while I haven't seen her in person for a few years, I still think about the impact she had on me quite regularly. What made them influential What made her stand out, I think, was that she genuinely refused to accept mediocrity from anyone in the room — not in an aggressive way, but in this quietly persistent, demanding kind of way that made you want to rise to the occasion. She had this incredible gift for asking questions that made you feel like you were thinking for the very first time — like she'd pulled a rug out from under an assumption you'd been standing on for years. I remember one lesson where she dismantled an essay of mine, sentence by sentence, and instead of feeling crushed, I walked out feeling genuinely excited to rewrite it. That was a rare quality. How they changed you What she fundamentally shifted in me was my relationship with effort. Before her, I think I had this fixed idea that you were either talented at something or you weren't — that it was essentially innate. She dismantled that belief completely, and replaced it with this conviction that rigour and curiosity are more valuable than raw ability. That's a perspective I still carry with me now, especially in my work. Rounding off So yes, I'd say she's probably the single most influential person in my intellectual life — more so, in some ways, than anyone I've met since.
    ✦ Band 9 · FC
    Specific, Named Introduction
    "A woman called Mrs Bensalem" — using a real-sounding name immediately makes the answer feel authentic and personal. Vague answers like "I'd like to describe a person" without naming them feel rehearsed. The time frame ("roughly a decade") is imprecise in a natural way — not "exactly 10 years."
    FC ↑
    ✦ Band 9 · LR + GRA
    Complex Characterisation
    "Quietly persistent, demanding kind of way that made you want to rise to the occasion" — this is multi-clause complexity produced fluently. "Rise to the occasion" is an idiomatic collocation used naturally. The nuance ("not in an aggressive way, but...") shows the speaker can qualify and contrast mid-sentence.
    LR ↑GRA ↑
    ✦ Band 9 · LR
    Extended Metaphor
    "Pulled a rug out from under an assumption you'd been standing on for years" — this extended metaphor is sophisticated, original, and vivid. It demonstrates the kind of creative, flexible lexical resource that distinguishes Band 9 from Band 7. Notice it's woven naturally into the sentence, not forced.
    LR ↑
    ✦ Band 9 · GRA + FC
    Reported Thought + Narrative Contrast
    "I had this fixed idea that... She dismantled that belief completely, and replaced it with..." — this past narrative structure uses a clear sequence (before → transformation → after). The contrast between past belief and new conviction shows both Grammatical Range (reported speech, past continuous) and strong Coherence (logical progression).
    GRA ↑FC ↑
    ✦ Band 9 · FC
    Natural Round-Off Phrase
    "So yes, I'd say..." is a conversational wrap-up that signals the speaker is concluding naturally, not because they've run out of language. The comparative "more so, in some ways, than..." adds a final nuanced dimension. Ending at 1:50–2:00 without being stopped = maximum fluency impression.

    📋 Part 2

    The POINT System — Structuring your 2 minutes

    Use this 5-step framework to map your 1-minute preparation. Each step generates roughly 20–25 seconds of content.

    POINT — The Part 2 Planning Framework

    Part 2 Essential
    P
    Person / Place / Thing — Identify and name it specificallyThe more specific, the more authentic. "A teacher called Mrs Bensalem" > "a teacher I had."
    O
    Origin / Context — When, where, how you know it/themSets the scene. Gives you 15–20 seconds of easy, fluent content.
    I
    Impact / Detail — The main content of the cue cardThis is your longest section (~60 seconds). Use specific memories and examples.
    N
    Nuance — A complexity, contrast, or reflectionAdd depth: "What surprised me was..." / "Although... in some ways..." This separates Band 7 from Band 9.
    T
    Tie-up — A final sentence that rounds off naturally"So yes, I'd say that..." / "That's why it's stayed with me." Never just stop mid-thought.

    Critical Part 2 Rules

    Structure Signals

    • Use clear signposting: "So, moving on to..."
    • Cover ALL bullet points on the card
    • Don't spend 90% on the first bullet
    • Use time markers to pace yourself

    Timing Rules

    • Aim to be stopped by the examiner (not finish early)
    • If you finish <60 seconds — you need more development
    • Use your 1 min prep: jot 5–7 keywords only
    • Don't memorise a script — examiners can tell

    +

    These phrases add the depth that separates Band 7 from Band 9. Use one per answer.

    What surprised me, though, was ...Although ..., in some ways ... It's funny, because I didn't expect to ... What I find interesting, looking back, is ... It's hard to say exactly why, but ... I think what I took from it was ...

    Q: Do you think teachers have become more or less influential compared to the past?
    That's a really interesting question, actually, because I think the answer is more nuanced than it might first appear. On one level, teachers have arguably lost some of the authority they once had — largely because young people now have access to a vast, essentially unlimited range of information and perspectives online, which means the teacher is no longer the primary gateway to knowledge. But I don't think that means teachers are less influential overall. If anything, the role of the teacher has shifted — from transmitting information to modelling how to think critically about it, which is arguably a more challenging and ultimately more influential role. The teachers who thrive in that environment, the ones who push you to question and evaluate rather than just absorb — those are the ones whose influence, I'd argue, is more profound than ever.

    The IDEA Framework — Answering Every Part 3 Question

    IDEA — The Part 3 Answer Formula

    Part 3 Essential
    I
    Indicate your position — with a hedge if needed Don't just say "I think yes." Signal complexity if it exists.
    "That's a nuanced one — I'd say yes, broadly speaking, though the way it's approached matters a great deal."
    D
    Develop — give your main reason Explain WHY you hold this view. Use "primarily because," "largely because," "the reason being that..."
    "The reason I say this is that young people increasingly form their identities through digital media..."
    E
    Elaborate — contrast, concede, or hypothesise Add analytical depth. Use "If anything...", "On the other hand...", "That said..."
    "That said, I think the relationship is more complex — it's not simply a case of X replacing Y, but rather..."
    A
    Assert — re-confirm your position End by restating your view clearly. Don't leave the answer hanging.
    "So on balance, I'd maintain that... though of course it depends greatly on context."

    Handling Difficult Questions

    When you don't know the answer

    • Use speculation: "I'd imagine...", "It seems likely that..."
    • Use hypothetical: "If I had to guess..."
    • Acknowledge complexity: "That's hard to generalise about..."
    • Never say "I don't know" and stop. Always continue.

    When you disagree with the examiner

    • Use polite disagreement: "I see where you're coming from, but..."
    • "That's a fair point, though I'd argue..."
    • The examiner may challenge you deliberately to test your confidence
    • Maintain your view — don't cave in just because they push back

    🏫 Reported March 2026, Part 3 · Linked to the Part 2 "money as a gift" cue card

    🎙
    Q: Why do people rarely use cash now?
    That's an interesting shift to think about, and I'd say it comes down to a combination of convenience and infrastructure, really. The most obvious factor is that contactless payment technology has become so seamless — you can tap a card or even just your phone, and the transaction's done in seconds, whereas fumbling around for the right coins always felt comparatively cumbersome. There's also, I think, a generational element to this: younger people especially have grown up never really needing cash, so for them digital payment isn't even really a "shift" — it's simply the default. That said, I do wonder whether this move away from cash creates problems for certain groups — older people, or those without reliable access to banking, for instance — who might find themselves increasingly excluded from everyday transactions.
    ✦ Band 9 · I (IDEA)
    "Comes Down To" — Analytical Opener with Two Named Factors
    Rather than a flat "I think it's because...", this phrase previews TWO factors (convenience and infrastructure) at once, giving the answer a clear analytical roadmap from the first sentence.
    ✦ Band 9 · D (IDEA)
    Second Factor Developed — Generational Framing
    Introducing a distinct second reason ("a generational element") and explaining it precisely ("isn't even really a shift — it's simply the default") shows layered reasoning rather than one flat explanation repeated.
    ✦ Band 9 · E (IDEA)
    "That Said, I Do Wonder Whether..." — Elaboration via Counter-Consideration
    This is the Elaborate step of IDEA: introducing a complicating social concern (financial exclusion) that most candidates wouldn't think to raise, showing genuine critical engagement with the question beyond the obvious answer.
    🎙
    Q: Is it good and necessary to teach children to save money?
    I'd say yes, absolutely, and arguably more necessary now than ever, given how frictionless spending has become with digital payments. The core skill children need to develop, I think, is an understanding that resources are finite — that money doesn't just appear infinitely whenever you tap a card. If anything, teaching a child to save early instils a kind of delayed gratification that extends well beyond finances; it's really a broader life skill about patience and long-term thinking. Having said that, I don't think saving should be taught in isolation — it's just as important that children learn WHY they're saving, whether that's for something specific they want or simply as a general safety net, otherwise the habit can feel arbitrary and may not stick into adulthood.
    ✦ Band 9 · I + A (IDEA)
    Immediate Position + Connected to Modern Context
    "Arguably more necessary now than ever, given how frictionless spending has become" connects the answer directly to the previous question's theme (cashless payments) — a sophisticated way to show the discussion is cumulative, not a set of isolated answers.
    ✦ Band 9 · D (IDEA)
    Generalising from the Specific to the Abstract
    "It's really a broader life skill about patience and long-term thinking" lifts the discussion from the narrow topic (saving money) to a more abstract, transferable concept (delayed gratification) — exactly the kind of analytical move Band 9 responses make.
    ✦ Band 9 · E (IDEA)
    "Having Said That" — Necessary Complication
    Rather than ending on a simple "yes," this final elaboration adds an important condition (saving needs a clear purpose) — showing the speaker has thought beyond the surface-level question to a more nuanced, complete answer.
